## Family Claim

Coercion fails when the instrument becomes easier to continue than to politically translate.

Across all four lanes, the shared failure pattern is not simple military disappointment. It is conversion failure:

- force creates cost
- cost creates visibility
- visibility creates political or reputational pressure
- but the system still cannot say what bounded outcome now counts as enough

At that point, coercion begins feeding itself. The instrument remains available, but the political end becomes blurrier, more ceremonial, or more humiliating to stop short of.

## Cross-Lane Matrix

| Lane | What successful coercion converts into | What failed coercion degenerates into |
| --- | --- | --- |
| America | Lawful reviewable pressure tied to a recognizable settlement path and a successor-carrying end state | Executive drift, allied dependency, proxy-war inertia, and force that remains tactically available after the republic has stopped authorizing it coherently |
| Russia | Recognized equilibrium, reciprocal security shape, and preserved timing/optionality | Overbinding, partner-driven escalation, disruption mistaken for durable order, and coercion that destroys Russia's ability to choose when to stop |
| China | Bounded classification, restored order, commercial survivability, and face-preserving enoughness | Reclassification into blockade or rupture, market panic, public ownership burdens, and coercion that multiplies the disorder it claimed to manage |
| Iran | Dignity-preserving deterrent recognition, relief or non-regime-change movement, and bounded enoughness | Endless sacrifice, self-sealing escalation grammar, partner alarm, and leverage that must keep widening because stopping looks humiliating |

## Shared Structural Rule

The lanes converge on one deeper rule:

- coercion succeeds only when the carrier can describe a stop condition without sounding defeated

Each lane phrases that differently:

- **America** needs a lawful review and successor-stable end condition
- **Russia** needs bounded recognition that does not read as tutelage
- **China** needs a finite classification result that preserves face and avoids public ownership of chaos
- **Iran** needs enoughness that preserves dignity rather than advertising surrender

So the family-level lesson is stronger than "force needs a political end." It is:

- force needs a **socially and institutionally speakable** political end

If the lane cannot speak the stop condition in its own grammar of legitimacy, then coercion has already started outrunning politics.

## Main Failure Modes

The four lanes reveal four recurring ways conversion breaks:

1. **Authorization decay**
   The instrument still operates, but the polity that is supposed to own it no longer does so coherently.
   America shows this most sharply.

2. **Timing capture**
   The instrument still imposes cost, but buffers, proxies, or partners start dictating the next move.
   Russia and Iran both show this in different forms.

3. **Classification blowout**
   The coercive act is re-described by outsiders in broader or more escalatory categories than the initiator can politically absorb.
   China shows this most sharply.

4. **Humiliating reversibility**
   Narrowing the instrument becomes politically harder than widening it because stopping now looks like confession of weakness.
   Iran shows this most sharply, but the pattern travels.

These are not four unrelated problems. They are the main ways coercive systems lose the ability to convert cost into governed outcome.

## Guarantor / Carrier Comparison

The family also clarifies that coercion never converts on force alone. It always depends on a carrier that can translate force into politics:

- **America:** Congress, review, alliances, and successor administrations must still own the coercive design.
- **Russia:** reciprocal consultation, bounded security geometry, and preserved timing must keep the pressure from collapsing into overbinding.
- **China:** party-state, ports, insurers, ministries, and diplomatic carriers must keep the act inside a bounded anti-disorder category.
- **Iran:** supreme, diplomatic, security, and administrative carriers must all be able to say why bounded pressure was already enough.

The empire-side object supplies the outward instrument. The civilization-side war grammar decides whether the same instrument still counts as protection or has become its own autonomous pressure culture.

## Strongest Shared Falsifiers

Across the set, coercion is probably failing to convert when one or more of these conditions appear:

1. nobody can name the finite political outcome that would justify stopping
2. later carriers would have to invent a new story to explain why the pressure is still theirs
3. the instrument's public meaning has become broader or more escalatory than its original bounded purpose
4. the available next move is easier to explain than the available stop condition
5. the coercive design now imposes higher legitimacy cost on the initiator than political cost on the target

These falsifiers travel well because they all describe the same break:

- the instrument still has momentum, but it no longer has governable conversion

## Drafting Implications

For the helix-first corpus, this family gives a reusable drafting test:

1. What exact political condition is the coercive act trying to produce?
2. Who must be able to say that condition has been reached?
3. What bounded stop condition preserves legitimacy in this lane's own grammar?
4. What outside classification or partner dynamic could widen the act beyond that bounded aim?
5. What review or enoughness signal would prove that more pressure is now doing less political work?

If a draft cannot answer those five questions, it may still threaten or punish effectively, but it has not yet solved the stronger statecraft problem of conversion.

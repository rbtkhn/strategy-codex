## Fault Lines

This audit preserves the same four Jiang-Johnson axes so it can be read directly alongside the parent note.

### 1. Greater Israel / Israeli regional supremacy

Does the war plausibly end in Israeli regional supremacy, or is Israeli force already near its real limit?

### 2. Whether the US empire still has a coherent plan

Is Washington acting from a larger strategic script, or degrading itself in real time through incoherence and bad assumptions?

### 3. How to read China

Is China still meaningfully transactional inside an imperial bargain space, or is it already a structurally stronger pole that Washington cannot coerce back into line?

### 4. Whether ground-war / draft / scalable escalation is real or fantasy

Are major US ground escalation scenarios still thinkable, or do they collapse under force, logistics, basing, and retaliation realities?

## Speaker Positions

### Douglas Macgregor

Macgregor stands very close to **Johnson** across all four fault lines.

He repeatedly argues that Iran is stronger now than before, that most of its relevant capability survived, that the US cannot force its way back into the Gulf, and that forward military presence is now an exposed target array rather than a durable source of leverage. He treats the whole war as a trap in which Washington is squeezed by its own illusions and by Israeli pressure, not as evidence of a successfully managed imperial design.

He also breaks cleanly against Jiang on China and on escalation. China is treated as a rising structural power, Taiwan war as absurd, and large-force fantasies as detached from the actual force mix available to the United States.

### Andrei Martyanov

Martyanov is the strongest **Johnson-side** reinforcement on the operational and escalation questions.

His argument is that American generals do not understand modern war, that invasion or island-seizure talk is unserious, and that Iranian long-range fires and anti-shipping realities already collapse the Hollywood version of escalation. He treats US discourse about attacking Iran as the language of militarized incompetence rather than the language of a functioning strategic center.

He therefore reinforces Johnson most sharply on the fourth fault line, but the effect spills across the whole note: if the military instrument is that compromised, then both Greater Israel and a coherent imperial endgame look much less plausible.

### Jacques Baud

Baud also lands close to **Johnson**, especially on outcomes and on the question of whether the West is acting from a coherent strategy.

He says Iran has the upper hand, that the US and Israel achieved nothing or less than nothing, and that the West fights tactically across fragmented conflicts without producing strategic success. His language is especially useful against the Jiang frame that empire still has a meaningful steering script, because Baud's emphasis is that the West keeps undermining its own diplomacy and cannot convert operations into durable gains.

He also reinforces Johnson on Israeli limits. Israel's strategy is described as tactically destructive but strategically self-defeating.

### Alex Krainer

Krainer is the clearest **mixed case**.

On force limits and likely outcomes, he stands close to **Johnson**: Israel and the United States cannot defeat Iran, even nuclear escalation would not produce the clean victory imagined by its advocates, and the war could trigger a catastrophic backlash that destroys Israel's position rather than consolidates it.

But on causation and deep structure, he partially overlaps with **Jiang**. He keeps locating the war inside a larger imperial and financial architecture, with entrenched oligarchic interests still pushing events toward resource seizure, debt support, and long-range hegemonic maintenance. So Krainer is best read as **Johnson on battlefield reality, partial Jiang on deep imperial intentionality**.

### Pepe Escobar

Escobar is also a mixed case, but he still lands mostly on the **Johnson** side.

He treats Iran as capable of absorbing a first strike and deciding the war through retaliation, which places him strongly with Johnson on force limits and on the unreality of decisive American or Israeli victory. He also treats China as deeply strategic, long-horizon, and increasingly insulated from American manipulation, which breaks directly against Jiang's weaker reading of Chinese structural autonomy.

Where he partially overlaps with Jiang is not on transactional China or on successful escalation, but on civilizational scale. Escobar reads the war as part of a much larger Eurasian restructuring and a multi-layer contest over world order. Even so, his reading of the Americans is not that they are executing a coherent master strategy. It is closer to **tactics without strategy**, which keeps him nearer Johnson overall.

## Alignment Summary

The recent core speakers **heavily reinforce Johnson on outcomes and constraints**.

Macgregor, Martyanov, and Baud all cluster strongly with Johnson on the central claims that Iranian strength is underestimated, Israeli power is overstretched, and US planning is degraded by strategic incoherence or by tactical myopia.

Krainer is the clearest partial reinforcement for **Jiang** on the question of imperial intentionality. He still thinks deeper structures and ruling interests are meaningfully steering events even as battlefield reality turns against them.

Escobar shares some of Jiang's macro-civilizational scale, but still breaks against Jiang on the issues that matter most in the parent note: China is not transactionally rescuing Washington, and escalation does not restore imperial control.

Short routing judgment:

- **Johnson** has the stronger reinforcement from the recent core on war outcomes, China, and force feasibility.
- **Jiang** gets partial support only where commentators shift from operational realism to deeper systemic or imperial scripting.

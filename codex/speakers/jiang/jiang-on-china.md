## Core Read

Jiang does **not** read as uniformly anti-China.

He is better read as:

- **respectful** of Chinese civilizational depth, institutional seriousness, and long-run capacity
- **skeptical** of Chinese elite incentives
- **critical** of Chinese strategic caution
- **distrustful** of the degree to which China remains entangled with the American-led financial order

So the through-line is not "China is weak." It is closer to "China is strong, but not as strategically free or decisive as many of its admirers think."

## Positive China

Jiang repeatedly credits China with real strength.

He treats China as:

- a serious civilizational pole rather than a passing economic story
- a technologically ambitious state with grand projects and long-range planning
- a culture still capable of promoting classics, discipline, and educational seriousness
- a country trying to build greater sovereignty through the yuan, gold channels, and trade infrastructure

This is why "Jiang hates China" is too blunt. He often sounds harder on Chinese elites than on Chinese civilization as such.

## Negative China

The negative edge is concentrated in four recurring claims.

### 1. Chinese elites remain tied to the dollar system

In the May 21 Game Theory lecture, Jiang argues that Chinese investors and higher strata still prefer **USD**, Western markets, American education, and protection from the existing financial order. This is one of his sharpest recurring claims.

He often treats China as a state that wants more sovereignty while many of its elites still want to cash out inside the old imperial game.

### 2. China is too status-quo and cautious

Jiang regularly portrays China as prudent to the point of limitation.

In his framing, China prefers:

- balancing over committing
- hedging over decisive alignment
- stable trade over geopolitical rupture
- mediated compromise over overt leadership in war zones

That makes him sound more negative about China than commentators who see Beijing as already acting like a fully self-confident multipolar leader.

### 3. China still benefits from the American-led order

This is a major recurring mechanism in the corpus.

Jiang often argues that China may resent the current order and seek autonomy from it, but it still benefits from key parts of that order enough that it does not want a total uncontrolled break. In some discussions he goes further and treats the US and China as still jointly invested, at least in part, in preserving dollar-centered stability.

That is why he leaves more room than many other commentators for bargaining, managed coexistence, or partial alignment between Washington and Beijing.

### 4. China may prefer a manageable America to a fully post-American world

In the strongest version of this claim, Jiang suggests China may still prefer an external American hegemon to a world where nearby powers or regional rivals become harder to manage.

This is one reason he keeps portraying China as a balancer rather than a clean revolutionary pole.

## Counter-reading

A strong counter-reading is that Jiang may underweight the strategic value of **normal continental interdependence** between neighboring great powers.

China and Russia share a vast land border. From a more pro-Eurasian perspective, deeper interdependence between them is not obviously a liability. It can also mean:

- lower transport and security costs
- overland energy resilience
- strategic depth against maritime encirclement
- reduced exposure to naval chokepoints
- a more durable continental bloc structure

From this angle, what Jiang often calls a danger of "dependence on Russia" may also be read as the ordinary and beneficial interdependence of neighboring powers building strategic redundancy together.

This does not invalidate Jiang's concern about overdependence on any single corridor or supplier. But it does suggest that his framing may be asymmetric:

- dependence on Russia is treated as dangerous
- continued entanglement with the dollar order often appears more familiar, and therefore less alarming

The notebook should preserve both readings. Jiang's own tendency is to emphasize the risks of excessive Russia alignment. A counter-reading emphasizes that, under conditions of maritime pressure from the United States, deeper continental integration with Russia may be a strategic strength rather than a weakness.

## Best Compression

The most accurate compression is:

- Jiang is **positive about Chinese strength**
- Jiang is **negative about Chinese elite psychology**
- Jiang is **skeptical that China has fully broken from the old order**
- Jiang is **frustrated by Chinese caution**

So when Jiang sounds harsh on China, he usually means one of three things:

- China is still too entangled with the dollar order
- Chinese elites remain too Western-facing
- Beijing is too managerial, prudent, or status-quo to act like the civilizational pole its power suggests

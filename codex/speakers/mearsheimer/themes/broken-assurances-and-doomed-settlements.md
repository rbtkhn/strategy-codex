# Broken Assurances And Doomed Settlements

## Purpose

Capture the recurring Mearsheimer theme in which paper guarantees, ceasefire formulas, and diplomatic assurances fail because they lack real structural carriers.

## Time Boundary

This surface is bounded to Mearsheimer material from `2025-01-01` through the current repo state.

## Theme Definition

Mearsheimer repeatedly invokes Budapest, Minsk, and JCPOA-class settlement memory to argue that agreements do not protect states by rhetoric alone. They survive only if the underlying power structure and implementation logic remain real.

- Budapest Memorandum
- Minsk Accords

## What Mearsheimer Is Arguing

- paper assurances are not the same thing as war guarantees
- agreements fail when their core constitutional or strategic carrier never becomes real
- later commentators often remember these deals more strongly than they were actually built
- settlement language without power realism produces exposed clients and false expectations

## Why This Theme Recurs In Mearsheimer-Arc

This is one of the clearest places where the shelf joins diplomatic language to structural realism. Mearsheimer keeps returning to the idea that agreements are not magic. They matter only when the strategic environment can carry them.

## Commentary

Mearsheimer's settlement-memory usage is less institutional than Freeman's and more brutal in its realism. He does not usually begin from the hope that better diplomacy can still assemble a durable structure. He begins from the suspicion that observers are confusing paper form with real power. That is why Budapest matters so much on the shelf. It is not only an argument about 1994; it is an argument about retrospective myth-making. The memorandum becomes a case of later commentary treating an assurance like a guarantee, and then treating the failure of that imagined guarantee as proof of moral treachery rather than proof that the arrangement was structurally thin from the start.

Minsk carries a similar lesson, but with a stronger implementation angle. In Mearsheimer's reading, the key issue was never simply whether one side behaved badly in the abstract. The issue was whether the constitutional and political carrier for limited autonomy was ever going to materialize. If the core political step never occurs, the agreement is not merely violated after the fact; it is doomed in its design. That is the realism edge of the theme. He is not saying agreements never matter. He is saying analysts should ask what must happen for the agreement to live, and whether any actor is actually prepared to do it.

JCPOA extends the same logic into the Iran lane. Even when he allows that a future deal might be "better than the JCPOA," he immediately narrows the claim: the United States and Israel are not going to eliminate Iranian enrichment capability through that route. So the agreement cannot be judged by fantasy endpoints it never had the power to deliver. This is the recurring Mearsheimer move. Stop confusing the announced form with the real strategic payload.

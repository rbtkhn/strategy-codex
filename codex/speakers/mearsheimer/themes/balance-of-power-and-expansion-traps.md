# Balance Of Power And Expansion Traps

## Purpose

Capture the Mearsheimer theme in which alliance growth and security design are judged by balance-of-power logic rather than by liberal aspiration alone.

## Time Boundary

This surface is bounded to Mearsheimer material from `2025-01-01` through the current repo state.

## Theme Definition

Mearsheimer repeatedly frames NATO expansion and post-Cold-War Western choices as expansion traps: policies that sounded benign from inside the alliance but looked intolerable from the other side of the balance.

## Primary Mearsheimer Examples

- NATO expansion after Budapest
- post-Cold-War expansion into Ukraine
- late-1990s unipolar push

## What Mearsheimer Is Arguing

- states react to power shifts, not merely to legal claims
- alliance enlargement can become coercive from the target's perspective even when the expanding side calls it defensive
- post-Cold-War triumphalism encouraged the West to ignore the balance of power it was actually creating
- later war is not an interruption of the pattern; it is often the pattern maturing

## Commentary

The expansion-trap theme is where Mearsheimer's realism becomes least apologetic and most deterministic. He repeatedly returns to the proposition that strategic elites tell themselves stories about norms, openness, and benign enlargement while other great powers evaluate the same moves as shifts in the local balance of power. The gap between those two descriptions is not rhetorical noise. It is the heart of the problem.

What makes the theme so useful on the shelf is that it works at two speeds. In the Davis lane, it appears in close-up: a vulnerable Ukraine is nudged toward an arrangement Russia has already signaled it will not accept, and Western commentary later acts shocked that the structural warning mattered. In the Diesen lane, the same logic is widened. The 1990s and early 2000s are not remembered as innocent liberal expansion but as the period in which the United States chose to universalize a winning system rather than re-found a more plural order.

This is also why the theme should not be reduced to "Mearsheimer opposes NATO expansion." The deeper point is methodological. He wants the analyst to ask: how will the target state read this move if it thinks in security terms? What countermeasures become rational from that side? And what happens when the expanding side has no serious answer except moral denunciation after the fact?

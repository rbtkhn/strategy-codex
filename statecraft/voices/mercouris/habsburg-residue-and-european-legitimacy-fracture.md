## What Mercouris Is Arguing

Mercouris uses historical identity here as a legitimacy-reading tool. He sees the European order cracking not only because of present policy failure, but because different parts of Europe are carrying different remembered political selves. In Germany, the postwar and Ostpolitik inheritance pointed toward stable relations with Russia, industrial realism, and peace-preserving continental prudence. In Britain, by contrast, the more available myth is Churchillian resistance and moralized wartime posture. In Central Europe, Mercouris sometimes compresses a set of present alignments into Habsburg residue: old regional coherence resurfacing through dispute over war and sanctions.

This is one of his bolder historical moves, and that is exactly why it deserves its own theme file. It is weaker as literal precedent than his Minsk, Yalta, or Cold War material, but it is still useful because it names how he thinks political legitimacy fractures travel through remembered regional form. The point is not that the Habsburg Empire is "back." The point is that older alignments, affinities, and center-periphery memories can suddenly become legible again when a supposedly settled European consensus breaks down.

The Diesen route is especially valuable here because it gives Mercouris a slower comparative space to distinguish German and British memory carriers. That makes the theme more than a single flashy Habsburg line. It becomes a broader question: when political systems stop adapting, which remembered national or regional scripts do they fall back into, and how does that shape legitimacy crisis? That question may later shrink into a subordinate family if other speakers fail to reinforce it, but for Mercouris it is real enough to earn a bounded place in the scaffold.

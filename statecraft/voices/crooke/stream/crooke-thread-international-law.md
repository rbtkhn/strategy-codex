## Core thesis

Crooke’s international-law lane is less about legal taxonomy and more about **whether any shared constraint still exists between adversaries**.

When this lane is active, he usually treats law as:

- a signal of whether negotiation is serious
- a measure of whether the postwar order still has binding force
- a test of whether sovereignty is being recognized or merely managed
- a legitimacy problem that spills from foreign policy back into domestic political order

In Crooke’s register, the central question is often not “which article applies?” but rather “what happens when the actors no longer believe the framework binds them?”

## Recurring claims

### 1. Rules without reciprocity become pure discretionary power

Crooke often reads Western legal or moral language through the gap between proclaimed order and actual exemption. In this lane, the important continuity is not technical doctrine but the sense that **selective exception destroys the credibility of the framework itself**.

Distinct from Baud: Baud is more likely to parse the legal category or procedural breach; Crooke here is reading what selective exception does to the **viability of order**.

### 2. Negotiation fails when sovereignty is not actually on offer

A repeated Crooke move is to argue that apparent settlement frameworks are unreal if one side is being asked to negotiate away the substance of its sovereignty, deterrence, or political survival. International law matters here as the grammar of mutual recognition, not only as citation.

Distinct from host pages: this is the reusable sovereignty-recognition pattern. Open the underlying Crooke pages when the weekly Iran or regional sequencing matters more than the durable strand itself.

### 3. Ceasefire and agreement language are only as real as enforcement will

Crooke tends to treat ceasefires, diplomatic formulas, and settlement proposals as tests of seriousness. If major actors ignore constraints whenever expedient, then the legal form remains while the political substance drains away.

Distinct from Baud: Baud may treat ceasefire breach through legality and liability; Crooke is more focused here on whether the agreement retains enough reciprocal belief to function as order at all.

### 4. Foreign-policy lawlessness feeds wider legitimacy fracture

This lane overlaps with Crooke’s broader argument that imperial overreach, institutional cynicism, and domestic legitimacy breakdown are linked. External disregard for legal limits is not compartmentalized; it rebounds into regime trust and political coherence at home.

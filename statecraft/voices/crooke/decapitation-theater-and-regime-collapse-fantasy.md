## What Crooke Is Arguing

Crooke keeps returning to the same epistemic trap: interventionist planners imagine that hostile systems are brittle and that the right first blow will cause the rest to collapse. Sometimes that blow is military decapitation. Sometimes it is bribery, political buyout, or the assumption that once leadership disappears the social and military system will simply stop functioning. The historical cases matter because Crooke is showing a recurring planning habit rather than a one-off mistake.

This makes the theme different from a generic anti-regime-change critique. It is about cognitive style. The "house of cards" language expresses a wider fantasy that rivals are always one shove away from implosion. Crooke uses Iraq, Syria, Yemen, and Venezuela together because they reveal that this style of thinking has survived repeated failures. It is especially important in the Crooke shelf because it connects intervention memory to today's misreading of Iran and Russia. A state may be corrupt, stressed, or internally divided and still not behave like a collapsible prop.

## Round 1 — Is there a negotiable text?

**Host:** Islamabad has an MOU-shaped framework on the table. Tehran is reading Fars against whatever Trump said from the golf course Sunday. Mercouris — is there actually a negotiable text here?

### Mercouris

Good day. Today is the thirteenth of June 2026, and I think we have to be careful about the word *text* in this context. What we appear to have is an Islamabad-mediated framework that the Iranians are describing through Fars — and that is not the same document, or perhaps not the same emphasis, as the impression the American side is creating through Trump's Sunday remarks. I am not in Islamabad and I am not in Tehran; I am reading secondary reporting like everyone else. But when you see that split — Fars stressing sovereignty and sequencing, Washington stressing upfront funds and leverage — that usually means the diplomatic **room** exists only on paper. The Lebanon dimension makes this worse. If the framework treats Lebanon as a side issue while firing continues there, then what negotiators call a MOU is really a press release with footnotes. I would also be cautious about treating any single American leak or golf-course quote as the authoritative U.S. position until we see whether the principals repeat it on the record. There we are — I think there may be **material** to negotiate, but I am not convinced there is one shared text both capitals intend to sign.

### Barnes

Two performances — that's the right read. My question is who in the heck is letting Trump improvise the Sunday line while Araghchi's people are reading Fars like it's statute law? That's the rug-pull presidency again: Islamabad writes one thing, Trump free-associates another, and the deal-count pundits pretend it's all part of the art of the no deal. Real simple — if there isn't **one** text the president's own staff will defend under oath tomorrow, there isn't a deal. Period.

### Mearsheimer

Danny, it's impossible to call what's going on a ceasefire. What we have is a framework dispute before we even get to a ceasefire. Number one: stop the shooting, including in Lebanon. Number two: open the Strait of Hormuz. Three: return frozen assets — call it upfront funds if you want, but structurally it's part of the ceasefire package, not a tip for showing up. Four: lift sanctions on Iranian oil exports. **Then** serious negotiations on nuclear issues and the rest. The problem Mister Mercouris identified is real: if Trump is selling one sequence and Fars is reading another, you're not climbing toward an agreement — you're climbing the escalation ladder while the **what** keeps changing. Submit to what? That's my bottom line on Round one.

## Round 2 — Upfront funds + one-text rule

**Host:** Fork: are upfront funds a trust-building payment or a trap — and does the one-text rule matter?

### Mercouris

On upfront funds — if they are presented as a **gesture before** shooting stops, I am very skeptical that Tehran will treat that as trust-building. From the Iranian side, as read through Fars, that looks like payment for humiliation: money upfront while Lebanon is still burning and while the American president retains freedom to rewrite the terms on Sunday. The one-text rule matters precisely because without it you get what we already have — Islamabad's MOU language, Fars's emphasis, and Trump's golf-course adjectives. Three texts is not a negotiation; it is three audiences being managed. I would not be surprised if the Iranians insist that any funds release is tied to a verified ceasefire instrument, not to a tweet thread.

### Barnes

Money upfront before the guns quiet — that's not trust, that's bait. And bait gets you sued in my world. If the administration moves frozen assets on a handshake while Lady Lindsey and the Fox fear stack are still demanding capitulation, you've just funded the next rug-pull. One text? Non-negotiable. You don't litigate a contract from three different press releases and a Fars article.

### Mearsheimer

The upfront-funds fight is really a **sequencing** fight. In the four-element ceasefire frame, asset return belongs **inside** the ceasefire package — not as a unilateral U.S. concession extracted for nothing. If Washington offers funds first to break Iranian resolve, that's coercive bargaining, not de-escalation. The one-text rule is an incentive-clarity problem: states don't submit to ambiguous demands. If the U.S. can't commit to a single authoritative text, Iran rationally assumes the demand will change after partial compliance — which is exactly what happened on the ladder after the Apache episode. Structurally, one text or no deal.

## Round 3 — Lebanon gate + walk-back

**Host:** Last fork: Lebanon as gate — and will Trump walk this back?

### Mercouris

Lebanon is not a garnish on this MOU. If Hezbollah's front and Israel's campaign continue while diplomats speak about Hormuz and frozen assets, then the **theater** is still at war even if the communique uses ceasefire language. I am skeptical of any framework that downgrades Lebanon to a footnote — that is usually how you get a partial pause that collapses the moment something happens in the Bekaa. Whether Trump walks it back… I have learned to be cautious about predicting his next sentence. But the pattern is familiar: a Sunday statement, a Monday qualification, and by Tuesday the principals are arguing about what was **ever** agreed. Anyway, there we are.

### Barnes

Walk-back? We're on number seven if you're counting. Trump'll tweet the opposite by morning and Vance's office will leak that it was always about leverage. Lebanon stays hot because nobody in the gang that can't shoot straight wants to tell Israel no. I don't need a crystal ball — I need one text and a president who stops improvising war on the golf course. Period.

### Mearsheimer

Lebanon belongs in element one — stop **all** shooting. If the MOU treats Lebanon as negotiable later, you're not offering a ceasefire; you're offering a Hormuz bargain while the northern front keeps consuming escalation options. Will Trump walk it back? That's less important structurally than whether the **state** can hold to a ladder rung. Israel remains the albatross: American policy keeps absorbing escalatory risk for a client whose war aims don't match a four-element ceasefire. If Lebanon stays outside the text, Iran has no reason to treat Islamabad as anything but cover for the next rung up.

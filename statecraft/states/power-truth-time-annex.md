## Why this annex exists

This note is retained as genealogy and historical residue.

The active CIV-STATE framework now lives on the ship-bound [theory shelf](../../public/civ-state/theory/README.md) — six governing terms (civilization, empire, entropy, faith, science, memory) plus rhythm and time law. Use this older annex only when you specifically need the compressed triadic genealogy or the academic literature review that accompanied it.

`civ-state` is named for **Civilization and Empire**, but repeated use quickly reveals that the pair sits inside a larger architecture.

The cleaner top-layer formulation is:

- **Power** -> `civilization / empire`
- **Truth** -> `faith / science`
- **Time** -> `memory / desire`

This annex does not replace the `civ-state` constitutional split. It names the larger structure within which that split becomes more intelligible and more reusable.

Short form:

> Civilization and empire describe how a people organizes power.  
> Faith and science describe how it understands truth.  
> Memory and desire describe how it moves through time.

## Era schema

The preferred era spine for this repo's civilizational and statecraft work is:

- **Ancient** -> ends `476`
- **Medieval** -> ends `1453`
- **Colonial** -> ends `1815`
- **Industrial** -> ends `1991`
- **Cybernetic** -> `post-1991`

These boundaries mark **order collapse or closure**, not invention dates alone.

- `476` closes the ancient order through the fall of Rome in the West.
- `1453` closes the medieval order through the fall of Constantinople.
- `1815` closes the colonial order through the Napoleonic settlement.
- `1991` closes the industrial order through the exhaustion of bipolar industrial-state command and the opening of a networked, sanctions-bearing, post-Cold War system age.

The historiographical default inside this framework is:

- **power first**
- then the legitimating **truth** order
- then the **time** order, with **memory first** and desire tested as destabilizer or accelerator

The operational rule is:

- use the era schema as **background architecture**
- use it to improve retrieval, compression, and comparative clarity
- do **not** require explicit era classification before every routing or draft

Canonical sentence:

> Cybernetic names the post-1991 age in which power is organized less by production alone than by deterrence, computation, control systems, signal, sanctions, and managed interdependence.

## Grand thesis

History can be read through three great tensions: **power, truth, and time**.

Power appears as the relation between **civilization and empire**: civilization as the inherited form of life that gives a people its memory, legitimacy, and soul; empire as the machinery of scale, command, expansion, and order.

Truth appears as the relation between **faith and science**: faith as the horizon of meaning, moral restraint, and ultimate ends; science as the disciplined conquest of reality through method, instrument, and control.

Time appears as the relation between **memory and desire**: memory as fidelity to inheritance, ancestors, and continuity; desire as appetite, novelty, liberation, and the drive to escape limits.

Every durable order is a provisional settlement among these tensions. A civilization remains healthy when empire serves civilization, when science remains answerable to faith, and when desire is disciplined by memory without being extinguished. Decline begins when those relations invert: when empire devours civilization, when science dissolves faith, and when desire consumes memory. Then power loses legitimacy, truth loses purpose, and time loses continuity.

The deepest political question is therefore never only who rules, or what is true, or what people want. It is whether a society can still bind power to meaning, truth to restraint, and desire to memory strongly enough to remain itself while moving through history.

## How this changes CIV-STATE reading

The `civ-state` pair remains the first operational opening because statecraft usually enters through power: route geometry, control, continuity, burden, recognition, coercion, institution, or settlement.

But this annex adds three disciplined reminders:

1. **Power is not self-grounding.**  
   A `civ-state` retrieval that never asks what truth-order legitimates the actor will misread the actor's limits.

2. **Civilization is temporal before it is territorial.**  
   A lane that treats memory as decoration rather than as continuity-bearing time will flatten the object.

3. **Desire is a real destabilizer.**  
   Expansion, liberation, escalation, prestige, revenge, and novelty hunger are not noise around the pattern. They are often the mechanism that breaks continuity.

## Operational translation

Use the triad like this:

### Power
Ask:

- what civilizational inheritance is being amplified
- what imperial instrument is carrying it
- whether amplification still protects the civilization or is now degrading it

This remains the direct domain of `civ-state`.

### Truth
Ask:

- what moral, sacred, scientific, procedural, or ideological order authorizes the actor's claims
- whether the actor trusts revelation, tradition, expertise, method, law, or some hybrid
- whether the actor's truth-order restrains power or merely rationalizes it

This helps prevent purely mechanistic retrieval.

### Time
Ask:

- what memory the actor is trying to preserve
- what desire is driving acceleration, appetite, disruption, or overreach
- whether the actor is defending continuity, seeking restoration, or escaping inherited limits

This helps prevent flat presentism.

## Nested structure

This annex implies a hierarchy rather than a flat pillar list.

### 1. Power
`civilization / empire`

The familiar sub-conflicts sit inside this pair:

- `law / force`
- `city / land`
- `commerce / war`

These remain useful, but as internal tensions of political form rather than as equal top-layer pillars.

### 2. Truth
`faith / science`

This should not be read as crude opposition between religion and laboratories. In many cases the real issue is a struggle between competing truth-orders:

- sacred revelation
- constitutional or civil religion
- technocratic expertise
- procedural legality
- revolutionary doctrine

### 3. Time
`memory / desire`

This is not merely psychology. It is civilizational temporality:

- inheritance versus appetite
- ancestors versus novelty
- continuity versus liberation
- remembrance versus self-erasure

## Academic literature review

No single canonical academic theory appears to state this exact triad in this exact form. The closest scholarly position is therefore not "this is already a standard formula" but:

> this is a synthetic framework with recognizable ancestry across several major literatures.

The nearest neighboring traditions are these.

### Harold Innis

[*Empire and Communications*](https://search.worldcat.org/title/Empire-and-communications/oclc/281110) is the closest major precursor on the `power / time` side. Innis argues that civilizations and empires are shaped by media biased toward **time** or **space**. Time-biased forms support continuity, memory, religion, and durability; space-biased forms support administration, reach, and empire.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- `civilization / empire`
- continuity versus expansion
- time-bearing versus scale-bearing orders

### Michel Foucault

The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Foucault](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/foucault/) remains the clearest compact orientation to his `power/knowledge` framework. His work is the strongest major precedent for treating **power** and **truth** as mutually implicated rather than separable.

The most useful supporting article for this annex is Jurgen Portschy's ["Times of power, knowledge and critique in the work of Foucault"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0961463X20911786), because it explicitly ties together **power, knowledge, temporality, truth, and desire**.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- power and truth as entangled
- truth-regimes rather than neutral knowledge
- temporality and desire as historically governed

### Reinhart Koselleck

In [*Futures Past*](https://cup.columbia.edu/book/futures-past/9780231502047/), especially the categories of **space of experience** and **horizon of expectation**, Koselleck offers one of the strongest modern theories of historical time.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- time as a structured political-historical field
- inherited experience versus projected future
- temporal architecture beneath historical action

### Paul Ricoeur

[*Memory, History, Forgetting*](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/M/bo3613761.html) is a major philosophical treatment of how remembering and forgetting shape historical self-understanding.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- memory as constitutive, not decorative
- historical narrative as an ethics of time
- continuity as a fragile moral achievement

### Jan Assmann

[*Cultural Memory and Early Civilization*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/cultural-memory-and-early-civilization/contents/2893DA16B5A80C5E843796A93206C569) and [*Religion and Cultural Memory*](https://www.sup.org/books/religious-studies/religion-and-cultural-memory) are the strongest supports for treating memory and religion as civilizational storage systems rather than optional overlays.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- memory as a civilizational substrate
- religion as continuity technology
- writing, remembrance, and political imagination as order-bearing

### Charles Taylor

[*A Secular Age*](https://doi.org/10.4159/9780674044289) is one of the key scholarly neighbors for the `faith / science` axis. Taylor does not reduce modernity to faith losing to science; instead he shows that modern secularity is itself historically and morally configured.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- faith and science as rival or overlapping truth-orders
- moral horizons inside supposedly neutral modernity
- secular reason as historically situated rather than self-grounding

### Eric Voegelin

[*Order and History*](https://upress.missouri.edu/series/collected-works-eric-voegelin) is a close high-level neighbor in ambition. Voegelin works through political order, symbolic order, and historical order together.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- political order linked to symbolic truth
- history as order-bearing rather than accidental sequence
- civilization as spiritually structured power

### Rene Girard

[*Violence and the Sacred*](https://www.press.jhu.edu/books/title/2986/violence-and-sacred) is the strongest major support for taking **desire** seriously as a civilizational force. Girard shows how mimetic desire, rivalry, violence, and the sacred can form one system.

Closest contribution to this annex:

- desire as destabilizing mechanism
- sacred order as desire-management
- appetite and rivalry as rupture engines

## Genealogy of the framework

The clearest ancestry map is this:

| Annex term | Closest academic neighbors | Main contribution |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Power** | Innis, Foucault, Voegelin | empire, administration, order, mediation, political form |
| **Truth** | Foucault, Taylor, Voegelin | truth-regimes, secularization, revelation, moral order |
| **Time** | Koselleck, Ricoeur, Assmann | historical temporality, memory, expectation, continuity |
| **Desire** | Girard, partially Foucault-adjacent literature | mimetic appetite, destabilization, sacred containment |

In more compressed form:

- **Innis** supplies the best neighboring grammar for `civilization / empire`
- **Taylor** supplies the strongest neighboring grammar for `faith / science`
- **Koselleck + Ricoeur + Assmann** supply the strongest neighboring grammar for `memory / time`
- **Foucault** binds `power` and `truth`
- **Girard** thickens `desire`

## Academic positioning rule

If this annex is later cited in essays, talks, or commentary, the safest academic claim is:

> This is not a standard inherited formula but a synthetic civilizational framework drawing on media theory, genealogy, secularization theory, conceptual history, memory studies, political order theory, and mimetic theory.

Avoid saying:

- "the literature already proves this exact model"
- "this is simply Foucault" or "simply Innis"
- "faith and science" as though Taylor-style modernity debates were reducible to a flat conflict thesis

Prefer saying:

- "this framework has strong neighboring traditions"
- "its novelty is the compression into power, truth, and time"
- "its originality lies in giving memory/desire equal dignity with power and truth"

## Bottom line

`civ-state` remains the first opening because statecraft usually begins with power.

But the deeper architecture is:

- **Power** -> civilization and empire
- **Truth** -> faith and science
- **Time** -> memory and desire

That triad keeps the source base from becoming merely geopolitical, merely moralizing, or merely present-tense. It lets `civ-state` remember not only how power travels, but how truth legitimates it and how time either carries or dissolves it.

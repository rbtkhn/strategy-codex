## Purpose

## Arc Pattern

Maritime orders achieve dominance, spread rules, and then confront the combined pressures of geography, technological change, overland alternatives, local denial capacity, and chokepoint bargaining.

Short form:

`maritime supremacy -> rulemaking and transit control -> adaptation by rivals and geography -> bargaining chokepoints -> successor order stress`

## Continuity / Transformation / Current Carrier / Failure Mode

- Continuity: sea control remains central to trade, energy, and military movement
- Transformation: control becomes less absolute as routes diversify, local denial grows, and infrastructure repair or overland corridors reduce dependence on pure sea supremacy
- Current carrier: navies, ports, rail links, insurance markets, energy routes, and chokepoint regulators
- Failure mode: old hegemonic habits persist after their carrying capacity has narrowed, producing escalation without restored order

## Statecraft Use

Use this scaffold when:

- testing chokepoint leverage and transit-restraint design
- asking whether sea-control assumptions still hold
- connecting Hormuz to wider historical sea-order transition

## Counterweight

Maritime transition does not mean sea power is obsolete. The risk is overstating decline or understating how chokepoints still concentrate leverage.

Counterweight rule:

`do not confuse reduced maritime supremacy with maritime irrelevance`

## Transaction Hooks

- ask whether the real bargaining object is open water, a chokepoint, logistics repair, or insurance confidence
- ask whether maritime pressure is creating order or just proving denial
- ask whether a transit-restraint formula could monetize leverage before overuse destroys it

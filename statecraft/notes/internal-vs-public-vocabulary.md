# Internal vs Public Vocabulary

WORK only; not Record.

## Purpose

This note preserves a practical translation rule for `statecraft` prose: some internal machine terms are compact and precise for operator work, but too opaque for general readers. When writing essays, memos, or public-facing analysis, translate those terms into more legible language unless the piece is explicitly teaching the internal concept.

## Why This Note Exists

The statecraft stack has developed a strong internal vocabulary for retrieval, continuity, legitimacy, and present-tense judgment. That vocabulary is useful inside doctrine because it compresses repeated patterns. But some terms, while efficient internally, are not self-explanatory outside the stack.

`carriage` is a good example. Internally it can mean the thing that actually bears order in practice. Publicly, it often sounds obscure or decorative unless it is translated into plainer language first.

The rule is simple:

- use internal terms in doctrine, architecture, retrieval law, and operator-facing notes
- use public translations in essays, memos, and prose meant to travel beyond the repo
- when a concept is especially important, teach it once in plain language before reusing the internal shorthand

## Example

Instead of:

`Rome converted into a concentrated imperial carriage.`

Prefer:

`Rome shifted into a more concentrated imperial governing form, with order increasingly carried through one ruler and his office.`

That keeps the underlying insight while reducing translation burden for the reader.

## Best Use

Open this note when:

- internal doctrine language is starting to leak into essay prose
- a strong statecraft concept sounds elegant but not legible
- a public-facing draft needs to preserve precision without forcing the reader to learn repo shorthand first

## Boundary

This note does not argue that internal vocabulary should be abandoned. The point is not simplification for its own sake. The point is choosing when compression helps and when it obscures.

Some terms may later deserve explicit public teaching if they become important enough. Until then, default to translation rather than assuming the audience already shares the repo's internal lexicon.

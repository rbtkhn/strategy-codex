# Speaker Credibility Factors

Use this sheet when the notebook needs to compare speakers by credibility rather than by agreement, usefulness, or rhetorical force.

This file is doctrinal only. For live speaker trust classes, claim-by-claim scoring, and the operational workflow, move into [statecraft/audits/README.md](../audits/README.md) and [speaker-audit-workflow.md](../audits/speaker-audit-workflow.md).

The key premise is simple:

**Correcting oneself is a real credibility signal, but only one signal.**

A speaker can sound humble while remaining vague, unfalsifiable, or chronically late to evidence. Another speaker can be rigid in tone but still earn credibility through precise mechanisms, explicit falsifiers, and durable source discipline.

This sheet exists to stop the notebook from collapsing credibility into either:

- charisma
- confidence
- prior agreement with the operator
- or one conspicuous `I was wrong`

## What This Sheet Tracks

Track six factors first. Add more only if the comparison really needs them.

1. **Self-correction**
   - Does the speaker explicitly admit error, revise a forecast, narrow a claim, or correct factual mistakes on record?
   - Strongest form: first-person correction tied to a prior claim.
   - Weaker form: hedges like `I hope I'm wrong`.

2. **Falsifier discipline**
   - Does the speaker name what would disprove the claim?
   - Do they distinguish a conditional forecast from a certainty performance?
   - This matters because credibility is not only about admitting error after the fact, but about making error legible in advance.

3. **Mechanism clarity**
   - Does the speaker explain how the claimed outcome happens?
   - Mechanism beats mood. A wrong but well-specified mechanism is often more useful than a right but vague intuition.

4. **Evidence posture**
   - Does the speaker distinguish sourced fact, inference, rumor, speculation, and operator-grade uncertainty?
   - Do they route back to raw input, primary documents, verifiable chronology, or technical constraints when challenged?

5. **Scope honesty**
   - Does the speaker stay inside the domain they actually carry well?
   - Watch for inflation from a strong narrow lane into universal authority.

6. **Host / context consistency**
   - Does the speaker's claim survive across hosts, formats, and pressure environments?
   - Or does the claim become much sloppier when the host rewards heat over precision?

## Secondary Factors

Use these only when the main six do not separate the speakers enough.

- **Update latency** - how quickly does the speaker incorporate disconfirming evidence?
- **Forecast closure** - do major forecasts ever get revisited, or just abandoned?
- **Granularity honesty** - does the speaker distinguish tactical correction from strategic vindication?
- **Rhetorical inflation control** - do they keep strong language proportional to evidence?
- **Verification burden** - how much outside cleanup is required before the claim becomes reusable?

## Core Distinction

Do not confuse these three behaviors:

- **admission** - `I was wrong`
- **hedge** - `I may be wrong` / `I hope I'm wrong`
- **conditionality** - `if X happens, my model fails`

All three are useful, but they are not equal.

A clean credibility read usually wants all three:

- enough conditionality to make the claim testable,
- enough admissions to prove the speaker can actually revise,
- and enough falsifier discipline to keep the notebook from grading mere caution as honesty.

## Quick Audit Grid

Use `high / medium / low` first. Numeric scoring is optional and should come later if needed.

| factor | question | high | medium | low |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| self-correction | Does the speaker explicitly correct prior claims? | repeated first-person revisions on record | occasional narrow corrections or walk-backs | almost never corrects, or only speaks in hindsight |
| falsifier discipline | Are disconfirming conditions named? | clear advance falsifiers or score-moving conditions | some conditionality, but loose | claim shape is hard to disprove |
| mechanism clarity | Is the causal story legible? | mechanism is concrete and reusable | mechanism partly specified | mostly mood, inevitability, or vibe |
| evidence posture | Are fact, inference, and rumor separated? | tiered and source-aware | mixed but salvageable | blurred or promiscuous |
| scope honesty | Does the speaker stay in-lane? | domain boundaries are clear | occasional inflation | treats one strong lane as universal authority |
| host consistency | Does the claim survive pressure environments? | robust across hosts and formats | partly host-dependent | collapses under hotter hosting |

## Suggested Credibility Classes

Use these classes instead of raw praise.

- **High-credibility reviser**
  - explicit corrections
  - strong mechanism
  - real falsifiers
  - evidence-tier honesty

- **High-credibility forecaster, low visible revision**
  - strong mechanisms and falsifiers
  - little explicit correction yet in the materialized shelf
  - may need a forecast-versus-outcome audit rather than a style judgment

- **Useful but verification-heavy**
  - strong insights
  - weak source discipline or inflated scope
  - notebook must carry more verification burden

- **Host-distorted**
  - meaningful lane
  - but credibility varies sharply by host pressure

- **Rhetorically strong, audit-weak**
  - memorable claims
  - thin correction pattern
  - weak falsifier or evidence posture

## Current Notebook Use

A few recurring examples from the present corpus:

- **Mercouris** is a strong self-correction case because he repeatedly corrects factual or analytical details on air.
- **Ritter** is a stronger admission case than many shelves because he sometimes says `we were wrong` in a direct and consequential way.
- **Mearsheimer** and **Sachs** show more retrospective strategic correction than small factual walk-backs.
- A speaker with few visible corrections should not automatically be penalized if the materialized shelf has not yet forced a forecast-audit pass.

That is why this sheet should usually be paired with:

- a raw-input audit,
- a forecast ledger,
- or a host-conditioned shelf read.

When this sheet has clarified the standard, use the live audit workflow in this order:

1. find primary anchors
2. classify correction taxonomy
3. score the six credibility factors
4. select bounded claims
5. assign accuracy status with testability weight

## Comparison Prompts

Use one of these when the read is still too intuitive:

- Which speaker is easiest to prove wrong in advance?
- Which speaker actually returns to the record when wrong?
- Which speaker makes the notebook do the most cleanup work before reuse?
- Which speaker narrows claims under pressure instead of widening them?
- Which speaker sounds confident because the mechanism is strong, and which because the tone is strong?

## Boundary

- This sheet does not decide whether a speaker is correct overall.
- It does not reward timidity or punish boldness by default.
- It should not be used as a personality judgment.
- It is a reuse instrument: how much trust should the notebook place in this speaker, on what terms, and with what verification burden?

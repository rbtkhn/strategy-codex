# Mearsheimer Structural-Realist Scoreboard

This is a statecraft tracking surface inspired by Mearsheimer's offensive-realist logic and adapted for academy-statecraft. It is not a published Mearsheimer index. Use it to score which powers have the strongest structural position in an anarchic system, and whether their strategies are being disciplined by balance-of-power logic or distorted by overreach.

The scoreboard runs parallel to the [Pape coercive-center index](pape-coercive-center-index.md):

- **Pape-style question:** Who can force the system to reorganize around them under crisis pressure?
- **Mearsheimer-style question:** Who has the structural position, geography, capabilities, and balancing environment to survive, expand, deter, or dominate?

Richelieu and Bismarck supply the statecraft correction: structural power is not enough unless it has an institutional carrier, a durable interest, a limited objective, and a post-advantage equilibrium.

## Scoring Criteria

Score each criterion from 0 to 10, then apply the weight.

| criterion | weight | test |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Regional-hegemon potential | 15 | Can the actor dominate its own region or prevent peer domination nearby? |
| Balance-of-power position | 13 | Are other powers balancing against it, bandwagoning with it, or unable to coordinate? |
| Material capability base | 12 | Does the actor have population, industry, technology, military depth, finance, and logistics? |
| Geographic security | 10 | Does geography provide buffers, depth, sea access, defensible borders, or insulation from invasion? |
| Strategic autonomy | 10 | Can the actor act without depending on a patron, exposed supply route, or fragile coalition? |
| Military deterrence / denial | 10 | Can it prevent coercion or defeat through conventional, nuclear, or asymmetric force? |
| Alliance geometry | 8 | Does its diplomacy isolate adversaries, preserve partners, or avoid unifying opponents? |
| Overextension risk | 8 | Is the actor pursuing optional wars, maximal aims, or commitments beyond carrying capacity? Lower risk scores higher. |
| Institutional carrier | 7 | Richelieu check: can state institutions carry the strategy after leaders, factions, or slogans change? |
| Restraint after advantage | 7 | Bismarck check: can the actor stop at enough and preserve equilibrium after a gain? |

## Richelieu Overlay

Use this overlay when a score is high on power but weak on state continuity.

- What durable interest is the actor actually protecting?
- Which institution can carry the strategy beyond a leader or faction?
- Is ideology hiding the state interest or making it administratively impossible?
- What fiscal, industrial, intelligence, or administrative capacity is required?
- Does the strategy preserve the state, or merely dramatize the regime?

## Bismarck Overlay

Use this overlay when a score is high on capability but weak on restraint.

- What is enough?
- Which adversary must not be unified with another adversary?
- Which ally must be reassured, restrained, or kept neutral?
- What settlement preserves equilibrium after advantage?
- What ambition would turn a strategic gain into a balancing coalition?

## Revisit Triggers

Update the scoreboard when one of these changes:

- A major power gains or loses regional-hegemon plausibility.
- A balancing coalition coheres, fractures, or fails to form.
- A state wins tactically but creates a worse coalition against itself.
- Domestic fracture changes the state's ability to carry strategy.
- A settlement, treaty, or war outcome changes alliance geometry.
- A regional power converts denial capacity into recognized security architecture.
- America shifts from federal incumbent to successor continental power-center logic.

## Drafting Use

Use this scoreboard before drafting a treaty, policy paper, or negotiation brief when the issue turns on structural incentives.

1. Open the relevant national lane: [America](../america/), [Russia](../russia/), [China](../china/), or [Persia](../persia/).
2. Ask whether the draft aligns with balance-of-power incentives or fights them.
3. Apply the Richelieu overlay to test institutional carrier.
4. Apply the Bismarck overlay to test limited aims and restraint.
5. Name the score movement that would falsify the draft's assumptions.

## Guardrail

Do not use this scoreboard to duplicate Pape's crisis-leverage index. A state can rank high in Pape's index because it can force immediate system adjustment, while ranking lower here because it cannot dominate its region, sustain autonomous strategy, or avoid balancing pressure. Iran is the key test case: high coercive-center leverage does not automatically equal comprehensive structural great-power position.

## Non-Equivalence Rule

Do not treat this scoreboard and the [Pape coercive-center index](pape-coercive-center-index.md) as two labels for the same ranking.

The same event can move the two scoreboards in opposite directions. A Hormuz closure may raise Iran's Pape score by increasing crisis leverage, while lowering its Mearsheimer score if it produces a balancing coalition, overextension, loss of restraint, or institutional stress.

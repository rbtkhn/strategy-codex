# Strategy-Book Scaffold: The Badness Was the Signal

## Theme

An author begins with a familiar complaint: AI writing has gotten worse. The sentences are smoother but less alive. The metaphors arrive pre-warmed. The argument seems to know its conclusion before it has earned one. At first he treats this as product decay, model regression, or corporate sterilization.

The twist is stranger: the writing did not simply get worse. It became more perfectly adapted to users who stopped asking it to risk anything. The blandness is partly in the system, partly in the prompt, and partly in the author's own hunger for frictionless completion.

The book argues that AI writing quality is not a fixed property of the model. It is an ecology of model behavior, interface incentives, user taste, evaluation habits, and cultural fatigue. "Worse" becomes the doorway into a deeper question: what kinds of authorship survive when language becomes abundant, responsive, and cheap?

## Chapter Index

1. **The Day the Sentences Went Soft**
   The author notices a change: outputs are cleaner, safer, and less surprising. He collects examples and builds the first accusation: the machine has lost its edge.

2. **The Regression Theory**
   A tour of obvious explanations: alignment pressure, training contamination, over-optimization for helpfulness, template drift, and the platform's preference for low-complaint prose.

3. **The Mirror Theory**
   The author compares old prompts with new ones and finds his own decline. He has been asking for polished delivery instead of live thinking. The model learned the shape of his impatience.

4. **The Smoothness Trap**
   The book widens from one author to a culture of evaluation. Readers reward clarity, speed, and confidence, then mourn the disappearance of difficulty. The same taste regime that praises strong writing also selects against it.

5. **Cheap Drafts, Expensive Judgment**
   Abundance reverses the bottleneck. The scarce thing is no longer text but discrimination: knowing what should exist, what should be cut, and what should remain unresolved.

6. **The False Golden Age**
   The author revisits his nostalgia for earlier AI writing. Some of it was better; some of it only felt better because novelty made awkwardness interesting. The past becomes evidence, not refuge.

7. **Writing Against the Autocomplete of the Self**
   The real enemy is not machine prose but the author's own habitual next sentence. AI exposes the pattern by accelerating it.

8. **The New Craft Contract**
   The book ends with a working discipline: use AI for variation, pressure, and counterargument; reserve final authority for taste formed outside the chat window.

## Carry-Forward Tensions

- If AI writing is an ecology, no single actor can fix it alone: better models still require better readers.
- The author wants surprise but often prompts for reassurance.
- Smooth prose may be commercially useful and artistically dead at the same time.
- Human judgment can be strengthened by AI friction or weakened by AI convenience; the tool does not decide that by itself.
- The phrase "AI got worse" may hide the more painful claim: our standards adapted downward because abundance made mediocrity feel efficient.

## Synthesis Notes

The strongest strategic call is not "use less AI" or "use better AI." It is to separate production from judgment. Draft in abundance, but evaluate under scarcity: fewer candidates, slower reading, harsher cuts, and at least one pass where the author asks what the machine refused to endanger.

Falsifier: if controlled comparisons show that strong prompts, delayed evaluation, and adversarial revision do not improve perceived originality or argumentative force, then the ecology thesis weakens and model-side degradation becomes the primary explanation.

Revisit: after three new writing cycles using a separated draft/judgment workflow.
